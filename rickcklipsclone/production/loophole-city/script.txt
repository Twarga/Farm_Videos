This town went broke, legally, thanks to one line of fine print.
You see, the city needed cash, so it issued bonds. IOUs you can trade.
And buried in the paperwork was a clause that let the buyer cash out early, at a higher rate.
Nobody thought it would matter. The rate looked safe.
Then the market moved, and the clause turned into a loaded weapon.
Every bond the city sold, the buyers could flip back at triple the price.
The city borrowed to pay the interest. Which made new bonds. Which made new interest.
It was a debt that ate itself. And grew.
Officials called it a liquidity event. The accountants called it a fire.
The mayor's office sold the water works, the parking meters, the street lights.
Anything that wasn't bolted down went on the block to plug the hole.
And the craziest part? The clause had been standard boilerplate for decades.
The people who wrote it never expected anyone to read it, let alone use it.
By the time they fixed the wording, the city had lost a generation of public works.
All because one paragraph said "subject to market adjustment" instead of "subject to market adjustment, capped."
One word. That's what it cost.
The loophole didn't break the city. The city broke itself, on autopilot.
